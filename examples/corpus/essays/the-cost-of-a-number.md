A number on a slide is a decision that has already been made. The work was in
choosing it, and that work happened somewhere off the slide, usually months
earlier, usually by someone who has since moved on.

This is not an argument against measurement. It is an argument for knowing
which number you picked and why, and for writing that down somewhere the board
can find it.

## Why boards love a number

A number ends an argument. That is its appeal and its whole problem. Ten people
who disagree about a strategy can agree about a figure, and the agreement feels
like progress when it is only the argument moving elsewhere.

The figure then acquires a history. It appears in three consecutive decks, and
by the fourth nobody remembers that it was an estimate made under time pressure
with two of the inputs guessed.

There is a version of this that works. The number carries its provenance: who
produced it, from what, with what confidence, and what would have to be true
for it to be wrong. Almost nobody does this, because it makes the slide ugly.

## The target problem

Once a number is adopted it stops describing the world and starts describing
the people being measured. Everyone knows this. Everyone quotes Goodhart at
each other and then adopts the number anyway, because the meeting has to end.

The practical defence is boring: keep two numbers that cannot both be gamed in
the same direction, and check them against something nobody is paid to move.

## What to do instead

Write the decision down before the number, not after it. If the figure came out
differently, what would you do? If the answer is nothing, the figure is
decoration and you are paying someone to produce it every month.
